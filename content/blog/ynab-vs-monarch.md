People compare these two constantly, and the comparison is a bit of a category error. They're both called budgeting apps and they cost about the same, but they're built for different problems.

Sorting out which problem you have takes about thirty seconds and saves you a wasted subscription.

We make a third kind of finance app, which makes us an odd narrator here — but it also means we're not trying to win this particular decision.

## The quick answer

| | YNAB | Monarch |
|---|---|---|
| Core method | Envelope budgeting — assign every dollar a job | Tracking, with budget limits on top |
| When it acts | Before you spend | After you spend |
| Changes behaviour | Yes, that's the point | Not really |
| Learning curve | Real | Low |
| Bank sync | Yes, or manual entry | Yes |
| Household sharing | Limited | Yes — built for it |
| Price at last check | ~$14.99/mo, ~$109/yr | ~$14.99/mo, ~$99.99/yr |

Prices move; check both sites.

## What YNAB actually is

YNAB is a method. The software exists to enforce it.

The rule is that every dollar you currently have gets assigned a job before you spend it — rent, groceries, the car insurance bill that's coming in March. You're not forecasting; you're allocating money that already exists. When you overspend a category, you don't get a warning, you get a decision: which other category are you taking that money from?

That friction is the product. It's why people who stick with YNAB talk about it the way they'd talk about a habit that changed something, and it's also why a lot of people bounce off it. The ceremony is constant, and if your finances are basically fine, it feels like a lot of admin for a problem you don't have.

## What Monarch actually is

Monarch connects to your accounts, pulls transactions automatically, categorises them, and shows you where the money went. You can set budget limits per category, track net worth, and — the feature it's genuinely best at — share the whole thing with a partner, each with your own login.

It's lower effort by a large margin. It's also less likely to change what you do. It's a mirror, with some guardrails.

## The actual decision

**Say the sentence that describes your problem out loud.**

- *"I keep overspending and I want to stop."* → YNAB. Nothing else on this page does that job.
- *"I have no idea where my money goes."* → Monarch.
- *"My partner and I need to see the same picture."* → Monarch.
- *"I want to save for specific things and stop raiding the fund."* → YNAB.
- *"I'm fine, I just want to keep an eye on it."* → Monarch, and don't overthink it.

The failure mode is picking YNAB when your problem was visibility. You'll do the setup, resent the maintenance, and quit inside two months — then conclude budgeting doesn't work for you, when actually you bought a treatment for a condition you didn't have.

The opposite failure is quieter: picking Monarch when you genuinely overspend. You'll get beautiful charts of the problem continuing.

## What they have in common

Both connect to your bank through an aggregator, so a third party holds a standing connection to your accounts. Both need periodic correction of miscategorised transactions. Both are subscriptions with no meaningful free tier.

If the bank connection is the part you object to, neither is your answer — YNAB can be run with fully manual entry, which some people do specifically for this reason, but that's a significant ongoing workload. File-based tools that read statements you download exist as a middle path, [including ours](/blog/budgeting-apps-without-bank-login).

## Running both

People try. The setup usually looks like YNAB for the method and Monarch for net worth and reporting.

It works for a month or two, then collapses, because you're categorising the same transactions in two systems and the second one stops getting maintained. If you want both jobs done, pick the one matching your *primary* problem and accept the other job being done worse.

## The honest summary

YNAB is a behaviour change you pay for. Monarch is visibility you pay for. Both are competently built and fairly priced for what they do.

If you can't tell which problem you have, that's useful information on its own — it usually means the answer is Monarch, because people who are genuinely overspending tend to know it.
